Hey,

Even worse — which ones are you still believing?

"95% of who we are by the time we are 35 is a memorised set of behaviours, emotional reactions, unconscious habits, hardwired attributes and perceptions that function like a computer program." — Dr Joe Dispenza

As I have just recently turned 30 (for the fifth time), this struck a chord with me. Being surrounded by some awesomely wise people has urged me to take a deep dive and shine a spotlight on myself in some dark and gloomy areas.

That age-old question: why do we do the things we do? Why do I reach for the comforting chocolate cookie or pasta, or avoid the situations that make me feel uncomfortable? If I'm to go forth and help people change their own forms, I need to figure out some reasons behind my own thoughts and habits. Are these sitting on the surface and easy to find? Nope.

The emotional response might be: I'm bored, stressed, nervous, scared. But why are those feelings there, and what is it about the thing you're reaching for or avoiding that's causing that response?

A personal example. June is hard for me. There was a traumatic experience, and each time it's triggered, my body wants the comfort of chocolate, cookies, ice cream or traditional Italian-style pasta. This has been apparent for eight years. It wasn't until I took a step back and really thought about why I actually craved this. I'm a PT now — I know this crap doesn't suit my body. So why was I reaching for it? Digging deeper, those were the foods my big sister and I would race home from school for, loading up big bowls to watch *The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air*. Those foods were my connection back to her — they took me to a safe, fun-times memory. That realisation hit me hard. I was driving at the time and had to pull over and cry it out. Your body doesn't know what's real; when you're rehashing a memory, the emotional response is huge.

So how do I deal with it now? In essence, I just wanted to create a safe space — nice memories to outweigh the June thoughts. We used to dance, sing and watch TV sitcoms. So now, when I start to feel the loss or want a sense of familiar comfort, I'll put on a song, a movie, or simply sing loudly in the car to the *Fresh Prince* theme. It may not be the most scientific approach, but if it stops me reaching for the chocolate that doesn't serve my goal, so be it. If I look like a lunatic singing in the car by myself — meh, who cares!

This brings me to avoidance: why do we avoid the things we do, and what stories are we believing about them? This came about when my mentor put sprint training into my program. My reaction: "I hate sprints", "I can't do sprints", "I'm not a runner". Yes, I whinge too! So I started the sprints. Were they hard? Yes. Was I enjoying them? No.

Mid-session — 10 by 100m sprints, with an Animal Flow round between — my mind wandered into why I'm so against running. I looked up to see a kids' school carnival across the park, and thoughts popped in: "I was never any good at sports carnivals", "I'm a swimmer, not a runner", "I've always sucked at running". It was that third one that struck a chord. Have I? What really makes me think that?

Boom — primary school sports carnival. I was a very overweight kid, 70kg at age 11. Carnivals were so much running, and I was forever last. My chest would wheeze with asthma, my face bright red, my thighs chafing. I felt slow, inadequate and heavy. I'd try so hard to run fast that I'd often fall over, because my body wasn't strong enough in the right places. I was never teased — that was simply how I felt about myself.

So in my two-minute break between rounds, I changed the story. I'm not that shell of a body anymore. This shell has worked hard and changed. This shell has quads, hip mobility, no asthma, and has done a half marathon. I told myself: I can do it, it's 100m, take it as fast as you can control, you've got glute control now — you've got this. So rather than avoiding the third round, off I went and finished it for the first time. Was it tough? Yes. Did I fall over? No. Had anything else changed — the distance, the environment, my energy? No. Only my thoughts about running. And that third Animal Flow round was the strongest of the three, at a point when I should have been totally fatigued.

So, the moral of the story: what habits are instilled in your "computer program" that you may not even realise? What stories have you been telling yourself that have stopped you from change? Do you avoid something that makes you uncomfortable? Why? How can you change the story?

Until next time, happy thoughts!
Nikki
